DSC 106 · Final project prototype · American Time Use Survey 2003–2024

Where does your day go?

Everyone gets 1,440 minutes. Twenty years of diaries from the Bureau of Labor Statistics show how Americans spend them, and how much less of that time we now spend with friends.

Team Data brokers: Aryen Palkar, Jayden Liauw, Haoyu Lin · Source code · Data: ATUS 2003–2024 multi-year microdata files (BLS)

Prototype, 18 Nov 2026. Charts 1 and 2 work; chart 3 is an early draft; the closing view is not built yet.

1

First, fill in your own day

Think about a normal weekday or weekend day. Drag each slider to roughly how many hours you spend on it. Whatever you don't assign goes into "everything else".

2

The average day hardly moves, except where it does

Each band is the average minutes per day spent on one kind of activity, from 2003 to 2024. Pick a group, hover a year to read the minutes, and hide sleep to see the smaller bands up close.

3

Draft: time with friends fell for every age group

Minutes per day spent with friends outside of work. Young people lost the most: people aged 15 to 24 went from about two and a half hours a day in 2003 to under one hour in 2024. Switch to "alone" to see the other side of it.

4

Coming next: your day, then and now

The closing view will put the reader's answers from step 1 next to the 2003 and 2024 averages for their group, so the last thing they see is where their own day fits in a twenty-year change.

Prototype write-up

What have you done so far?

We downloaded the BLS American Time Use Survey multi-year files for 2003 to 2024, which hold 252,808 diaries, and wrote a Python script (prep/build_data.py) that groups the 400 or so activity codes into about a dozen categories and computes survey-weighted average minutes per day for every year, age group and job status. We checked some of the results against numbers BLS publishes, like about 9 hours of sleep and 2.6 hours of TV a day in 2024, and they line up. The page is laid out as one scrolling article. The "fill in your day" sliders work and compare your hours with the 2024 average for your age group and job status, and the stacked area chart from 2003 to 2024 works with dropdowns for age and job, a hover readout for each year, and a toggle that hides sleep. We also have an early version of the time-with-friends chart, because the respondent file already has each person's total minutes alone and with friends, which turned out to be easier than working through the "who" file like we planned in the proposal.

What will be the most challenging part of the project to design, and why?

The hardest part will be the stacked area chart. Most of the day barely changes from year to year, so when the chart shows all 24 hours, losing 20 minutes of socializing is a thin band getting slightly thinner and nobody will notice it. Hiding sleep helps, but we still have to decide whether this chart should show the change directly, maybe as the difference from 2003, or just set up the friends chart that comes after it. The opening is the other hard part, because the sliders have to be quick enough that people actually fill them in, and the comparison has to say something useful even when someone's day looks nothing like the average. We also have to show 2020, when BLS stopped collecting diaries for about two months, without making it look like a real change in how people spent their time.

Minutes are survey-weighted means (BLS final weight TUFNWGTP; TU20FWGT for 2020). Ages 15 and older. 2020 has no diaries from 18 March to 9 May because collection stopped during the pandemic.
